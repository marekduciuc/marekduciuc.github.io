The situation

Since 2026 I have been on a contract with a software company, working on service desk tickets and on integrations between its business systems: the service desk, Power Automate flows, Zendesk, Monday.com and Jira.

Support work is full of context: what the customer said last week, which flow was changed, what was already tried. An AI assistant helps only if it keeps that context between sessions – and only if it cannot break anything while it helps.

What I built

A ticket workspace in the terminal

  • Memory per ticket: each ticket gets a folder with a short current summary, a full append-only history of every finding, decision and action, the proposed solution and the attachments. A journal across all tickets records what each session did.
  • Focus mode: the assistant loads one ticket at a time, syncs it from the service desk and reports what changed since last time. Several sessions can run side by side, each holding its own ticket.
  • Knowledge base: reusable fixes are filed by system and searched when a new ticket comes in.
  • Private notes and new tickets: it drafts internal notes and new tickets; nothing is sent until I confirm.

A Power Automate specialist

  • Analysis first: before anything is built, it gathers the existing tables, columns, environment variables and connection references, writes an analysis, and a critic agent challenges it. Reuse comes before anything new.
  • Build with a review loop: up to three rounds of build, lint and review against the original request. If the third round still fails, I decide.
  • Deployment to development only: it imports into the development environment and verifies the result. Acceptance and production are read-only for it.
  • Documentation: each flow is documented, checked against its actual definition, scanned for secrets and proposed as a pull request.
  • Zendesk changes: webhooks, triggers and automations change only through a guarded gateway, with a backup first and one confirmation per change.

A tool that improves itself – with my approval

A review command reads the ticket histories and the journal and proposes improvements to the tool itself, each with the evidence behind it. One example: the service desk dropped line breaks in notes, so a note arrived as one unreadable block. The review spotted it, proposed a formatting rule and a permanent fix in the API gateway, and I approved both.

How the work changes

  • The assistant watches: it checks the service desk for new replies on a ticket and reports what changed.
  • The assistant proposes: it suggests how to solve the ticket and drafts the reply.
  • A person decides: the assistant only writes drafts. A person reviews every reply and is always the final say.
  • Every solution is kept: fixes go into the knowledge base, so the next similar ticket is solved faster.
  • Integrations end to end: for a Power Automate request it can build the whole flow – analysis, build, review and a tested deployment to development.

How it stays safe

  • Allow-list, not trust: the gateway to the service desk knows only the permitted actions – reads, a private note, a new ticket from a confirmed draft. It never changes an existing ticket's status or fields.
  • Deletes only its own work: it can delete only notes it created itself, and only after an explicit yes.
  • Hooks that block: deleting files, raw web calls and edits to protected files are blocked; histories and the journal are append-only.
  • Secrets stay out: passwords come from the macOS Keychain and are never shown; no tokens or trigger URLs are written to ticket files.
  • Human checkpoints: I approve the analysis, decide after failed build rounds and confirm every deployment and every write outside the service desk.
  • Text for people reads like a person: e-mails and Teams messages are refused until they have gone through a plain-language pass.

Results

  • Both plugins are in active use in the IT support department: every ticket and every Power Automate change keeps its own memory, so work continues across sessions.
  • An integration change between a project board and the support desk went from analysis through a tested build in development to production, verified with a smoke test after I promoted it.
  • The review loop proposed 10 improvements to the tools: 8 went in, 2 I rejected.
  • Packaged as an installable plugin marketplace with setup guides, so colleagues can install the same tools and get updates automatically.

What I learned

The review loop matters as much as the first version. The tool sees its own mistakes in the ticket histories – a formatting problem, a missing check – and proposes the fix with evidence. Keeping the final decision with me is what makes that safe: I rejected two of the ten proposals, and they did not go in.

What this means for your team

  • Give the AI a memory in plain files, so work continues across sessions and people can read it.
  • Allow-list what it may do in each system instead of trusting it to be careful.
  • Keep production read-only for the AI; a person promotes.
  • Let it propose improvements to its own process, and review every one.
